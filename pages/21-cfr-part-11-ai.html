What is 21 CFR Part 11?

In short, it's the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)'s set of rules for making sure that electronic records and signatures are just as trustworthy, reliable, and legally binding as paper records and handwritten signatures. If your company works in a regulated industry (like pharmaceuticals or medical devices) and uses computer systems to manage data, these rules apply to you. This guide breaks down each part of the regulation.

Subpart A: General Provisions

§ 11.1 Scope

The Official Text

"The regulations in this part set forth the criteria under which the agency considers electronic records, electronic signatures, and handwritten signatures executed to electronic records to be trustworthy, reliable, and generally equivalent to paper records and handwritten signatures executed on paper..."

In a Nutshell

This rule applies when you use electronic systems for records that the FDA requires you to keep. It defines when the FDA will accept your electronic records and signatures as official, just like paper documents.

§ 11.2 Implementation

The Official Text

"(a) For records required to be maintained but not submitted to the agency, persons may use electronic records in lieu of paper records... (b) For records submitted to the agency, persons may use electronic records in lieu of paper records... provided that: (1) The requirements of this part are met; and (2) The document or parts of a document to be submitted have been identified in public docket No. 92S-0251 as being the type of submission the agency accepts in electronic form."

In a Nutshell

You can use electronic records instead of paper ones for both internal documents and submissions to the FDA. However, for submissions, you must ensure the FDA has officially stated they accept that specific type of document electronically.

§ 11.3 Definitions

The Official Text

Defines terms like "Act," "Agency," "Biometrics," "Closed system," "Digital signature," "Electronic record," "Electronic signature," "Handwritten signature," and "Open system."

In a Nutshell

This section is a dictionary for the rule. It clarifies key terms to ensure everyone is on the same page. For example, a "closed system" is one where you control who has access (like your company's internal network), while an "open system" is one where you don't (like the internet).

Subpart B: Electronic Records

§ 11.10 Controls for closed systems

The Official Text

Lists controls including: validation, accurate record generation, protection of records, limiting system access, use of audit trails, operational system checks, authority checks, device checks, training, written policies, and system documentation.

In a Nutshell

If you control your electronic system (a "closed system"), you need a bunch of safeguards. Think of it as a checklist: prove the system works correctly, keep accurate copies, protect the data, control who gets in, track all changes (audit trails), and make sure everyone is trained and follows written rules.

§ 11.30 Controls for open systems

The Official Text

"Persons who use open systems... shall employ procedures and controls designed to ensure the authenticity, integrity, and, as appropriate, the confidentiality of electronic records from the point of their creation to the point of their receipt. Such procedures and controls shall include those identified in § 11.10, as appropriate, and additional measures such as document encryption and use of appropriate digital signature standards..."

In a Nutshell

If your system is "open" (uses the internet), you must do everything required for closed systems, PLUS extra security measures. This usually means encrypting your data to protect it while it's being sent, ensuring it's not tampered with along the way.

§ 11.50 Signature manifestations

The Official Text

"(a) Signed electronic records shall contain information associated with the signing that clearly indicates all of the following: (1) The printed name of the signer; (2) The date and time when the signature was executed; and (3) The meaning (such as review, approval, responsibility, or authorship) associated with the signature..."

In a Nutshell

When someone signs an electronic document, the signature must appear clearly. It needs to show the person's full name, the exact date and time of the signature, and why they signed it (e.g., "Approved," "Reviewed," or "Authored by").

§ 11.70 Signature/record linking

The Official Text

"Electronic signatures and handwritten signatures executed to electronic records shall be linked to their respective electronic records to ensure that the signatures cannot be excised, copied, or otherwise transferred to falsify an electronic record by ordinary means."

In a Nutshell

The signature must be permanently attached to the specific record it signs. You can't be able to copy and paste the signature onto a different document. It needs to be securely bound to its original record.

Subpart C: Electronic Signatures

§ 11.100 General requirements

The Official Text

"(a) Each electronic signature shall be unique to one individual and shall not be reused by, or reassigned to, anyone else. (b) Before an organization establishes... an individual's electronic signature... the organization shall verify the identity of the individual. (c) Persons using electronic signatures shall... certify to the agency that the electronic signatures in their system... are the legally binding equivalent of traditional handwritten signatures."

In a Nutshell

Every person gets their own, unique electronic signature—no sharing! Before giving someone a signature, you have to confirm their identity. Finally, you have to formally tell the FDA that you consider these electronic signatures to be as legally binding as a wet-ink signature.

§ 11.200 Electronic signature components and controls

The Official Text

"(a) Electronic signatures that are not based on biometrics shall: (1) Employ at least two distinct identification components such as an identification code and password... (b) Electronic signatures based on biometrics shall be designed to ensure that they cannot be used by anyone other than their authorized owners."

In a Nutshell

For a standard electronic signature, you need at least two ways to identify the user, like a username and a password. When a signature is used, the person must enter both components. If you use biometrics (like a fingerprint), the system must be built to ensure only the right person can use it.

§ 11.300 Controls for identification codes/passwords

The Official Text

Lists controls for passwords/codes: ensuring uniqueness, periodic password changes, loss management procedures, safeguards against unauthorized use, and checking for intrusion attempts.

In a Nutshell

This section details how to manage passwords and user IDs securely. It requires things like making people change their passwords regularly, having a plan for when someone loses their password, and monitoring the system to detect and prevent hacking attempts.
